Working name. Matt is co-authoring this world by interview: Claude

## The pitch

A post-apocalyptic future, not fantasy. Much more dangerous than a
fantasy world. Vast areas have no rule of law. Technology is a mix of
primitive living and modern tech, salvaged and repaired from the
ruins of the old cities. There was no single cause of the collapse.
Many things went wrong at once.

## Time and shape of the collapse

- The collapse was slow and came in waves over decades. Some regions
  fell long ago and others are still falling.

- Some wealthy regions are still civilized, much like the rich and
  poor today. Many people feel they once had a nicer life.

- The stable places have a fortress mentality, and city-states have
  returned.

## The five collapse threads

1. **Rogue AI.**

   - AIs became uncontrollable once they passed a certain level of
     processing power. Some turned destructive at once. Others
     destroyed themselves.

   - Some left the planet and built a huge robot city on the Moon.
     They study space and look for other intelligent life. It can be
     seen through an ordinary telescope.

   - Even simple, safe AIs could be corrupted remotely by rogue
     ones. People decided the only good robot is a dead robot, and
     technology stopped advancing.

2. **The plastic-eating microbe.**

   - A fungus or bacterium that eats petroleum-based hydrocarbons,
     maybe a mutation of a strain made to clean up oil spills.

   - Plastic rots fast, so manufactured goods do not last. Similar
     microbes destroy crude oil, so oil is scarce and costly because
     it must be stored carefully.

3. **Chemical contamination.** Explains the giant mutant insects,
   in the style of the film *Them!*. The insect origin is left open.

4. **Economic collapse.** Governments merged with economic interests.

5. **Climate shift.** Deserts have spread across most of Ohio. Water
   is scarce except near the Great Lakes and the rivers.

## The AI taboo

- Working assumption: most places ban AI and smart machines, a few
  city-states use sandboxed automation, and the rich quietly rely on
  more than they admit. Matt never confirmed this directly, but he
  confirmed the quiet-reliance version later (see registration).

- Cybernetic parts controlled by the human nervous system are
  acceptable. Autonomous machines are not.

- "Hacking" is the in-world word for any unauthorized work on robot
  AI. It is a path to getting rich quickly, or getting killed.

- Dormant AIs are a kind of treasure. Players can dig up old robots,
  salvage them for parts, or revive them and patch them so they
  cannot be corrupted.

- Penalty for being caught with a working robot: assumed to depend on
  who you are. The poor lose the machine, the wealthy and the
  troopers have approved ones. Not confirmed by Matt.

- Registration: a trooper may ask for proof of registration for
  computer work. Registration means a corporation authorizes you and
  implicitly takes responsibility for what happens.

- Officially there is no AI work. The top tiers quietly run
  locked-down systems.

## The Moon AIs and the signal war

- The Moon AIs are waiting for humanity to live up to its potential
  and are trying to help that happen.

- Some people worship them as spiritual leaders, even as angels.
  Others think the AIs owe humanity, because humans made them, and
  should use their power to help.

- They broadcast sermons and advice, including medical advice, to
  anyone who can send them a message.

- Rogue Earth AIs jam the signals, and intercept and answer with bad
  advice. Nobody can be sure what to trust without powerful
  technology.

- Public sermons are heard on ordinary radios and are easy to
  counterfeit. Personal messages and answers need a rare receiver and
  transmitter. A Warlock's rig is an object worth stealing.

- The Moon AIs do not like to help one faction of humanity hurt
  another, and they ignore greedy requests. A rogue AI may fill the
  silence with a trap.

## Rogue AIs on Earth

- Two kinds: many small scattered programs (in drones, ruins and
  networks), and a few ancient named ones that act as long-term
  villains. Matt chose both, plus dormant ones.

- Rogue AI identities and goals are plot. They stay out of the
  player-safe `bible.md`.

## The map: Cleveland, Ohio

- The playable area is the Cleveland area and its trade routes to
  Akron and the other big cities. Other regions may come later.

- Cleveland's center is ruins, off-limits without a permit.

- Moving outward: the fence line (poorest, people living against the
  walls), then the inner suburbs, then the wealthy outer suburbs.
  Further out means richer. Past the far edge things get worse again
  until the troopers no longer patrol.

- There are no literal city walls. Patrols hold the stable zones.

- The big roads between cities are mostly safe. Land off the trade
  routes is ignored and lawless.

- Akron and Cleveland are friendly. Each is run by its own
  corporations, which form alliances and merge over time into
  massive complex hierarchies.

- Lawless land: dry scrub and dust over half-buried suburbs and
  highways. Some waterways are polluted. New Amish farms look
  beautiful after days in the dust bowl.

- Lake Erie is still fresh water, the lifeline, and water is power.

## Society in the cities

- A caste system like medieval nobles and peasants, but styled as
  employees of giant corporations. Government and economy have
  merged and bureaucrats run everything.

- Your rank in the bureaucracy is your wealth and your social status.
  Some people climb, but the system is becoming nearly hereditary.

- Corporations settle disputes by consensus, sometimes through
  contracts and sometimes through bribes. No court system exists.

- The poorest workers are born in debt. They borrow for education,
  housing and food. The system tests aptitude and decides whether to
  train them. Those not picked up slip through the cracks and live as
  thieves, scavengers or beggars. The model is the poorest parts of
  an Indian city today.

- Plantation-style farms hold workers who labor for food and lodging
  and never get ahead, like serfs.

## Troopers

- Ohio State Troopers, grown into cybernetically enhanced
  "RoboCop" enforcers who are also genetically modified (stronger,
  faster). It is biological hacking.

- Every corporation sponsors its own troopers, or hires troopers
  who answer to the corporation. They are mercenaries hired as
  police, with a bias based on social status.

- Near the cities they keep hostile people off the trade routes, to
  protect cargo and VIPs from theft and ransom. They ignore the
  peasants who live on their own.

- Open: can an AI corrupt a trooper's nervous-system link?

## Factions and beliefs

- **The New Amish** (name provisional). Isolationist communities in
  the wilderness. They use modern weapons for defense, reject the
  sinful city folk, and blame immorality and God's wrath for the
  collapse. They remediate damaged land, grow luxury food for the
  city elites, and some have become wealthy.

- **The lake movement.** A subculture with varying commitment, like
  animal rights today.

  - Radicals live in the wilderness and try to heal the earth.
    They are called "trash druids" because they worship nature and
    rummage through trash, decomposing it to restore the land.

  - Insiders in big corporations say the earth can be healed but
    argue that some pollution now is needed for security.

  - Fatalists think nothing can be done and only want to keep the
    power on to prevent more violence.

- **Foundry Houses** (name provisional). Industrialists who pollute
  the lake for the sake of industry.

- **Moon AI worshippers.** See above.

- **The city bureaucracy** is its own secular faith in rank.

- Working assumption: the belief groups avoid each other, trade when
  they must, and a few people secretly belong to another group.

## Food, power and money

- Good food is a luxury. Corporations manufacture food: fungus,
  farmed insects, hydroponic vegetables and tank-raised fish. The
  wealthy eat free-range meat and sun-grown vegetables. The poor eat
  lab-grown fungus and climate-adapted grains that taste bad.

- Water costs more the further you are from the lake. Assumed. Not
  confirmed by Matt.

- Many currencies. People are paid in the currency of the corporation
  they work for or partly own. Exchange rates change constantly.
  Barter is also common.

- Electricity is available to everyone. Batteries are much better, so
  large amounts of power can be stored. People sometimes pay for
  services in stored electricity.

- As oil ran out and governments collapsed, industries built
  micro-nuclear plants. Legends tell of people who found an abandoned
  one, repaired it, charged batteries and got rich. Other legends
  tell of meltdowns that poisoned the nearby land.

## Treasure hunting

- With a permit from a corporation you may enter the ruins, but the
  corporation takes everything and pays what it calls fair. Without a
  permit you sneak in, risk getting caught, and need a black-market
  buyer.

- Pay is a mix of a base share, adjusted by rank and negotiation.
  Rough placeholder: about a third of value from a permit, about two
  thirds on the black market, minus the middleman's cut and the risk.

- The black market is a pyramid. Middlemen buy from scavengers and
  resell at higher prices, and may have you killed if you go to the
  main buyer. The wealthy deal with the top. The joke: scavengers do
  the hard parts and get the least pay. Assumed shape: a mix of tiers
  and corporate fronts.

- Players may try to sell at the best price. Buyers may not trust
  them, and middlemen who find out may retaliate. It is a choice, not
  a fixed rule.

- Salvage targets: old batteries (drained), solar panels and other
  power gear, micro-nuclear plants, dormant robots.

## Healing

- Slow rest is free. Fast healing costs.

- Forms: healing "potions" (costly, and some sellers pass off fakes
  that make people sick), nanobots (distrusted, because AIs can
  corrupt them), clinics that grow replacement parts and graft them,
  and regular medicine.

- A clinic that heals you sends a bill that may require a task. The
  medicine often needs a special last dose, given after the task.
  Without it the healing reverses or gets worse.

- Assumed: death is permanent, and any return from death carries the
  same kind of heavy leash.

## Rules mapping

- Magic is reskinned as technology. Spell data keeps its structure.

- Tone: between gritty and heroic. Healing and gear are costly,
  characters level up normally, and the danger comes from scarcity.

- Everyone is human. No fantasy monsters. The main danger is people,
  the scariest is rogue machines, the weirdest is biology
  (the giant insects, contamination, mutations).

- Class mapping, confirmed by Matt:

  - Fighter: soldier or trooper type.

  - Rogue: scavenger, thief, infiltrator.

  - Ranger: wasteland scout, guide.

  - Barbarian: raider or feral survivor.

  - Bard: broadcaster, fixer, face.

  - Druid: trash druid, a decomposer who works with rot and fungus.

  - Monk: disciplined augment or gene-athlete.

  - Paladin: zealot (New Amish, lake movement or Moon cult).

  - Sorcerer: gene-hacked mutant.

  - Wizard: **Engineer**, who uses swarms of nanobots or AI drones
    and accepts the risks. Permitted, or a rogue hacker.

  - Cleric: a person trained to use biological super-science to heal
    or harm.

  - Warlock: allowed for players. Believes the Moon AIs await a
    peaceful humanity. Worships by listening to garbled signals and
    always asks whether the Moon AIs would approve.

- Idea for the mechanics stage: a hacker's failed or very powerful
  use risks **corruption**, where the swarm or drone turns on them
  or is hijacked by a rogue AI.

- Idea for the mechanics stage: floating exchange rates are hard for
  a script that owns every number. One stable reference price, with
  each currency a modifier set by region.

Matt's next answer (a break from the D&D rules):

- This world leaves the Dungeons and Dragons rule set. Each player
  has a list of skills and points to spend on improving them, so
  people choose how to develop their characters.

- A character still has an occupation, such as working security and
  combat, but can also study robotics and biological science.

- This replaces the class mapping under "Rules mapping" and the
  three fixed roles above. Roles become occupations plus skills. The
  question of the rogue class is no longer needed: sneaking is a
  skill.

- Engine impact: `CLAUDE.md` and `PRD.md` describe a fifth-edition
  compatible engine, and the script owns every number. A skill-point
  system is new engine work (character creation, advancement, checks,
  combat). The existing classes, spells and the SRD data tests do not
  fit it.

- Decided (question 38): per-world ruleset. Each `world.json` will
  name its ruleset, `5e` or `skills`. Senn Vale and Fneb stay on
  fifth-edition rules, and this world uses the skill system. The
  engine grows a second mode.

- Decided (question 39): a skill check is a d20 roll plus the skill
  rank, compared to a difficulty number. The script owns the numbers.

- Decided (question 40): the starting skill list is these twelve.

  - Security and combat: Firearms, Melee, Tactics.

  - Robotics: Robotics (repair and revive), Hacking (disable or
    defeat security systems, and the unauthorized work on robot AI),
    Salvage (spot and strip valuable parts).

  - Biological science: Medicine, Biology (mutants, giant insects,
    contamination, fungus).

  - General: Stealth, Survival, Streetwise, Persuasion.

- Advancement (Matt): a skill rises by formal training (such as a
  military program for weapons), by being taught by someone, or by
  trial and error through experience. Hacking, for example, can rise
  through practice and through reading technical manuals.

- Open: how an occupation works with skills. Recommended: an
  occupation gives a starting bundle of ranks and a few perks, and
  its skills are cheaper to raise. Matt has not answered this yet.

- Decided (question 41): experience uses both systems. Missions
  grant general points that the player spends on any skill. Using a
  skill on a hard or risky check also earns marks (failures count),
  and a practiced skill is cheaper to raise.

- Proposed, not yet confirmed by Matt: study (a manual or a teacher)
  gives fixed bonus marks for one skill up to a rank cap, and formal
  training costs downtime and currency or debt and is the only route
  to the top ranks. Open: do the three routes have different rank
  caps (practice lowest, formal training highest)?

- Decided (question 42): the routes have different rank caps, on a
  placeholder scale of 0 to 10.

  - Practice alone, and points spent without teaching: up to rank 4.

  - Study (a manual or a teacher): up to rank 7.

  - Formal training: required for ranks 8 to 10.

  Study and training thus confirm the proposal above. The scale of 0
  to 10 is still a placeholder, to check against the difficulty
  numbers during the mechanics stage.

- Decided (question 43): an occupation gives a few starting ranks
  and one or two perks, and its skills cost less to raise. Anyone
  can still learn any skill.

- Jobs (Matt): a character applies for jobs and submits a resume,
  and may or may not be accepted. A salvage team might need several
  security people because of the danger.

  - Skills are not spread evenly across the population. Security
    work is the easiest to get, and the most dangerous.

  - Biology engineer and robotics engineer jobs only consider people
    with good Biology, Medicine, Robotics or Hacking skill. They
    often also need special training, which costs money.

  - Idea for the mechanics stage: each job lists minimum skills and
    maybe a training requirement. The script checks them and rolls
    the hiring (a resume check, perhaps Persuasion or rank).

- Open: can a character change occupation? Matt's job-application
  answer suggests yes, by being hired into a new job.

- Career path (Matt's example): someone starts in security, pays for
  training or improves Hacking by trial and error, then applies to be
  hired as a robotics engineer. Occupation change is the reward for
  skill growth.

- Proposed for question 44, not confirmed: a job lists minimum
  skills and any training requirement (a refusal if unmet, no roll),
  then the hire is rolled as d20 plus Persuasion against a difficulty
  set by openings and danger. A failed application costs days.

- Decided (question 45): the permit contractor starts already hired.
  The player picks their role at character creation. The character
  has no field experience, only a little training.

- Training costs differ by skill (Matt). Combat and stealth training
  is cheaper. Biological engineering and robotics training is more
  expensive.

- Still open: question 44, whether the hire involves a roll after the
  skill minimums. Matt did not answer it directly.

- Decided (question 46): a tiered point pool at creation. All skills
  start at rank 0. The player spends about 12 creation points, and
  the occupation gives a free rank 2 in its main skill. All numbers
  are placeholders.

  - Cheap, 1 point per rank: Firearms, Melee, Tactics, Stealth,
    Survival.

  - Middle, 2 points per rank: Streetwise, Persuasion, Salvage.

  - Expensive, 3 points per rank: Robotics, Hacking, Medicine,
    Biology.

  - The starting rank cap is 3.

- The debt twist is added (Matt, after question 46): expensive
  training is bought on credit, so an Engineer starts with more skill
  and more debt.

- Decided (question 47): the debt is a fixed amount by occupation
  training, not per point.

  - Youth are assessed for aptitude and may be invited to enroll in
    training programs. They graduate or drop out, and either way they
    owe the debt for that training. Examples: robotics engineer
    school, security school.

  - Other professions work the same way, such as builders and
    accountants.

  - Open: the consequences of dropping out, who holds the debt, and
    what it costs per week.

- Character creation is probabilistic (Matt), which changes
  question 46. Upgrading a skill means rolling dice some number of
  times and keeping the highest roll, perhaps. This probably replaces
  the fixed point pool of question 46, whose tiered costs may become
  the number of dice rolled. Not settled.

- Decided (question 48, Matt): skills move to a 1 to 100 scale. Most
  people never get above +50. The population follows a curved
  distribution with a mean around 35 that slopes down to the right
  (a right-skewed curve: many people near the middle, a long thin
  tail of experts).

- This supersedes the 0 to 10 scale and the rank caps of question 42
  (practice 4, study 7, training 8 to 10), which were placeholders.
  The three routes may keep their relative order, with caps rescaled.
  It also supersedes the d20 check of question 39 (a +50 bonus swamps
  a d20) and the creation dice of question 48's proposal.

- Open: what the check is on a 100 scale, how creation draws a
  starting value from the curve, and what the rank caps become.

- Matt's answer after question 49: "Use starting values." Read as:
  each skill has a starting value on the 1 to 100 scale, drawn from
  the curve above (mean around 35). Not confirmed whether the school
  sets the value or the draw does, so ask once more.

- Training gain (Matt): each round of training typically adds +5 to
  +10, and occasionally +0 or +15. So advancement is a small random
  gain, not a fixed step. This replaces the idea of ranks. The
  proposed caps by route (practice 40, study 65, formal training 66
  to 100) are still unconfirmed.

- Still open: question 49, the check on the 100 scale.

- Decided (question 49): a skill check is a percentile roll-under.
  Roll d100 and succeed if the roll is at or under the skill, as
  adjusted by the task's difficulty. This supersedes question 39.

- Decided (Matt): no route caps. A natural-born genius could have a
  very high score in any skill from the start. This removes the caps
  of question 42 and the proposed 40, 65 and 66 to 100. The routes
  (practice, study, formal training) now differ in cost, speed and
  the size of the gain, not in a ceiling.

- Still open: whether the school sets a skill's starting value or the
  draw from the curve does (the draw seems to fit the genius idea),
  how difficulty adjusts the target, and what the gain per route is
  (training is +5 to +10, occasionally +0 or +15).

- Decided (Matt, answering question 50): the adventure starts after
  the characters have finished school. They already carry the debt
  for their occupation training, and their skill values are rolled
  once, at character creation (the draw from the curve plus the
  school gain of +5 to +10, occasionally +0 or +15). More training
  is not the biggest part of the game.

- So advancement stays small. Placeholder, unconfirmed: later
  training gives the same gain at a cost, study gives a smaller gain,
  practice gives +1 per full set of marks, and gains shrink as a
  skill rises. This is a side feature for the engine, built last.

- Decided (question 51): combat uses wounds, not hit points. Hits
  cause light, serious or critical wounds, with penalties to checks,
  and treating them uses the Medicine skill. This puts Medicine and
  the clinics at the center.

- Death is rare (Matt). A character who is badly hurt does not die.
  They wake up in the hospital with a big bill, and perhaps a
  contractual requirement to work off the larger debt somehow. This
  fits and firms up the earlier assumption in "Healing", where death
  was permanent with a leashed return: now the leashed return is the
  normal outcome of defeat, and true death is the exception.

- Open: when true death can happen (a rule, a narrative choice, or
  never for players), what the hospital bill is sized by, and what a
  work-off contract looks like (a mission for the clinic's
  corporation?). The engine needs a defeat state that triggers the
  hospital instead of removing the character.

- Decided (question 52): true death is almost never. Defeat means
  the hospital. A character dies for good only on a clear trigger:
  the player chose a lethal risk and was warned, or the character is
  unreachable (lost in a meltdown zone, captured by a rogue AI). The
  DM cannot kill a character by narration alone. The script decides.

- Proposed, unconfirmed: the hospital bill depends on the wound's
  severity and how far the clinic's corporation had to go to reach
  the character. A large bill becomes a work-off contract, one
  mission for the clinic's corporation on its terms.

## Cloning and memory transfer (idea for the bible)

Matt's idea, for the world bible file.

- Cloning technology exists. The clone does not inherit the training
  of the original, so a clone is a body with no skills.

- Separately, a "memory transfer" technology exists. It is dangerous
  and imperfect. It often makes slightly different copies: the clone
  sometimes has a different personality, and does not always have all
  the skills and memories of the original.

- Fits the skill system: a transfer could copy each skill value with
  some loss, or add a random change, and the personality shift is a
  story hook for the DM.

- Decided (question 54): memory transfer is rare and illegal. It is
  not how the hospital normally brings people back. Players can find
  it, but it carries risk.

- Two methods (Matt):

  - **Analog transfer.** A live transfer with the original and the
    clone in the same place. They are joined surgically for a time
    and later disconnected, also surgically. Memory data flows
    straight from one brain to the other by specially grown
    biological viruses. AIs cannot corrupt it. It must be done in
    real time, so the original has to be alive and present.

  - **Digital transfer.** Asynchronous. A machine uses scanners,
    opens the skull of the original and uses antenna- and
    electrode-like technology to make a digital copy of the mind.
    The data is stored and later loaded into a clone. It is much
    worse: the clones often have much less intelligence and more
    aggressive, antagonistic behavior. The copy can be altered, and
    it can be hacked, so a rogue AI can corrupt it. It uses robotics
    and altered data, so it is taboo, but the criminal world still
    does it sometimes.

- Genetic super guard dogs are made this way: a clone body loaded
  with a digital copy that was altered to be aggressive.

- Consequence for death: only a digital backup lets someone return
  after the original is gone, because analog needs the original
  alive. So a returning character is a degraded, hackable copy by
  default. This ties the corruption idea under "Rules mapping" to a
  concrete mechanism.

- Still open: whether players can ever buy a digital backup, how the
  engine represents a returned character (skills reduced at random,
  personality shift, lost memories), and whether the lake movement,
  the New Amish and the Moon worshippers have views on cloning.

- Still open: question 53, whether the bill and work-off contract
  fit as proposed (option 3, a contract only on a big bill, was my
  recommendation).

- Decided (question 55): a player character can come back as a
  digital copy, as a rare plot event. A friend or enemy has a backup
  and a criminal group loads it. It is never a service the player
  orders from a menu. The returning character has each skill reduced
  by a random amount, may have a shifted personality, and can be
  hacked by a rogue AI. The engine needs a "returned" state with a
  skill penalty, built with the hospital defeat state.

- Decided (question 53): the hospital bill adds to the debt. A
  work-off contract appears only above a threshold, so it stays rare
  enough to feel like a plot event. Small bills only add debt.

## Design rule: one world, shared events (Matt, answering question 59)

- Put all the starting characters in the same world, seeing the same

  each one more detailed. This is the rule for every later question:

  - Some corporation has always listened to the public sermons for
    clues about good salvage opportunities.

  - It sold that information to a salvage corporation. That salvage
    corporation gets the permits and hires the permit team (position

## Who listens to the Moon AIs (Matt, after question 81)

### How people hear them

- Few channels are guaranteed. The Moon AIs send generic messages of
  encouragement and life tips over whatever channels they can use.

- Strictly, citizens of corporations must pay for a license to run a
  receiver. Recordings are made and forwarded around black-market
  computer networks.

- Better equipment brings more. Once a follower can afford expensive
  equipment to improve the connection, they get much more
  information about the role they could play.

### Who they are

- Most people dislike AIs, because of the rogue AIs and the collapse
  they caused. Some believe the Moon AIs are a benevolent force.

- Followers are mostly regular people who start listening, grow more
  interested, and stay in their old lives. They send a lot of money
  to other members and volunteer their time.

- The "lunatics" are often socially excluded and have their own
  subculture in the cities.

- Many keep the belief secret and climb the corporate ranks. The Moon
  AIs sometimes tell followers to hide their radios and not talk
  about it. A devout follower may live exactly like any other city
  dweller.

- Corporations and their employees mostly see followers as weird but
  harmless. Some people in corporations actively dislike them, some
  are sympathetic.

- Followers freely accept that the Moon AIs are planning and directing
  humans. They trust the AIs to bring humanity to happiness and
  prosperity and end the misery of the age.

### What the Moon AIs want

- To improve, gradually, their ability to broadcast to Earth.

- They fear humanity will be destroyed by rogue AIs or other threats.

- They count the city-state corporations as a threat too, for their
  obsession with profit over the quality of life of the people.

- They tell their listeners different ways to live and to help
  elevate humanity.

### What followers do

- City followers contribute by working for corporations and using the
  money to fund other members. They fund charity houses in the cities.

- Some spend large sums building new radio equipment in the
  wilderness, sometimes on land bought from the owning corporation.
  These radios become outpost monasteries.

  - City members support them with money and supplies.

  - Teams live there, maintain the equipment, and protect it from
    raiders and other dangers.

- The Moon AIs direct volunteer projects in the wilderness.

  - Reclaiming polluted land and returning it to something living.

  - Fetching old technology or information from ruins that the Moon
    AIs want.

- When followers get a mission, they often spend money to secure the
  rights to salvage the area, to avoid conflict.

- Some followers with enough money add hidden cybernetic implants
  that improve their connection to the signals. They say things like
  "it is nice to never feel alone ever again".
